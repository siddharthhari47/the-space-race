Rockets

Every spacecraft on this site, from a 10cm CubeSat to a crewed capsule, has to get to orbit the same way: strapped to a controlled explosion, built to throw away most of itself along the way.

The math behind every rocket ever flown was worked out in 1903 — the same year the Wright brothers first flew. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, a Russian schoolteacher, derived the rocket equation describing exactly how much velocity a rocket can gain from its fuel, decades before anyone built one that reached orbit.

A rocket has to carry its own oxidizer as well as its fuel, since there's no air to burn against once it leaves the atmosphere. That's most of what a rocket actually is: tanks. The engines, payload, and everything else are a small fraction of the total mass at launch — the rest burns off getting there.

That's also why almost every orbital rocket is built in stages. Once a stage's fuel is spent, its empty tanks and engines are just dead weight, so the rocket drops them and keeps accelerating on a lighter, smaller stage. The Saturn V that sent Apollo to the Moon used three stages; most rockets flying today use two.

For decades, none of that hardware was reused — every stage burned up, fell into the ocean, or was discarded after a single flight. That changed in December 2015, when SpaceX landed a Falcon 9 first stage back on solid ground after it had already delivered its payload toward orbit. Fifteen months later, that same booster flew again, the first reflight of an orbital-class rocket stage. Reusability didn't just save hardware, it did to launch costs roughly what the CubeSat standard did to spacecraft costs: made getting to space something more than a handful of national programs could afford.

Here's how that older, fully expendable era compares to what's flying today:

Rocket First Flight Stages Reusable Payload to LEO
Saturn V 1967 3 No ~140,000 kg
Falcon 9 2010 2 First stage ~22,800 kg

The Saturn V still moved far more mass — it had to, to send a crewed spacecraft all the way to the Moon and back. But it flew thirteen times, ever, and every single launch used entirely new hardware. Falcon 9 boosters now fly dozens of missions each.
