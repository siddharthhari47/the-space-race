Rockets

Getting to space is easy. A hundred kilometres straight up is a big firework. Getting to orbit means arriving with about 7.8 kilometres per second of sideways speed, and that is where nearly all the difficulty and nearly all the propellant goes.

02

Why it throws most of itself away

Try it in the panel above. Push the stage-1 propellant right up and leave stage 2 tiny, then do the opposite. The second arrangement wins, and it is not close.

Once a rocket is above the atmosphere there is no air to burn, so it carries its own oxidiser alongside its own fuel. That is most of what a rocket is: tanks. And the moment a tank is empty it stops being a fuel tank and becomes luggage.

The rocket equation only cares about the ratio of full mass to empty mass. Dry structure sits in the denominator forever, so every kilogram of spent tank you keep carrying is a kilogram permanently taxing everything you do next. Dropping it resets the ratio for the stage that follows — which is why this vehicle gets about three kilometres per second more out of the same propellant than a single stage would.

This exhibit holds structure at a fixed fraction of propellant — 5.5% for the first stage, 4.5% for the second — so the propellant sliders move a real mass ratio rather than a free parameter. Those are close to Falcon 9's actual numbers: about 22 tonnes of dry stage on 411 tonnes of propellant.

03

Height was never the problem

Load the vehicle up until it fails, and read what the failure actually says.

An overloaded rocket still gets to space. It will happily sail through a hundred kilometres, past the Kármán line, comfortably into vacuum — and then fall back down, because it never got going sideways fast enough. Orbit is not an altitude you reach. It is a horizontal speed you survive long enough to acquire.

Roughly 7.8 km/s of that is the orbit itself. The rest — about 1.5 to 2 km/s on a real launch, and the simulation above reports its own figure every flight — is lost to gravity while the vehicle is still climbing, and to dragging a fuselage through thick air on the way out. Add them up and low orbit costs somewhere near 9.4 km/s of ideal delta-v. That number is the whole design brief.

The Saturn V moved far more mass, because it had to send a crewed spacecraft to the Moon and back. But it flew thirteen times ever, and every launch used entirely new hardware. Falcon 9 boosters now fly dozens of missions each.

04

And then they stopped throwing it away

For decades none of that hardware came back. Every stage burned up, fell into the ocean, or was written off after one flight. That changed in December 2015, when SpaceX landed a Falcon 9 first stage on solid ground after it had already delivered its payload toward orbit.

That particular booster never flew again, as it happens — it was retired and put on display outside SpaceX's headquarters in Hawthorne. The first actual reflight came in March 2017, using a different booster recovered from a mission the year before. That is the moment reusability stopped being a demonstration.

The maths behind every rocket ever flown was worked out in 1903, the same year the Wright brothers first flew. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky, a Russian schoolteacher, derived the relationship between propellant mass and achievable velocity decades before anyone built a machine that could reach orbit. The panel at the top of this page is still solving his equation.
