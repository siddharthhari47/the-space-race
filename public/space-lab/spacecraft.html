Spacecraft

Getting to orbit is a rocket's job. Keeping a person alive once they're there, and getting them back through the atmosphere in one piece, is the spacecraft's, and arguably the harder half of the problem.

Early heat shields, including Vostok's, worked by ablation: the outer material was designed to burn away and flake off deliberately, carrying reentry heat with it rather than letting it soak into the capsule. It's a strategy that treats losing material as a feature, not a failure: the shield is only doing its job if a visible layer of it is gone by the time the capsule lands.

Coming back from orbit means shedding an enormous amount of speed as heat. The heat shield's outer surface often hits around 1,650°C during reentry, hot enough to destroy most materials outright without a way to manage it. That's on top of everything else a satellite never has to deal with: a breathable atmosphere to maintain, heat from the astronauts themselves as well as the sun, and a person who has to arrive at the other end still alive.

Vostok 1 carried Yuri Gagarin into orbit in 1961, the first time any spacecraft had carried a person at all. It was simple by necessity: a spherical descent capsule, an ablative heat shield, and very little automation the cosmonaut could actually override. Six years later, in 1967, the Soviet Union introduced Soyuz, a more capable, more controllable design that, after a difficult early history, became one of the most reliable crewed spacecraft ever built. Soyuz is still flying today, over half a century after its first launch.

Modern spacecraft look different from either. SpaceX's Crew Dragon, which began flying astronauts in 2020, replaced Soyuz's mostly-analog cockpit with touchscreens and a much higher degree of automation, while keeping the same fundamental job: protect the crew, survive reentry, land safely.

Crew capacity barely moved across sixty years, from one seat to four. That's not because engineers couldn't fit more people in. Fitting more people in was never actually the hard problem — surviving the trip, and doing it again on the next flight, was.
