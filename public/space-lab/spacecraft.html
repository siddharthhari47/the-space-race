Spacecraft

Inside a crewed spacecraft, breathable air, drinking water and temperature control are engineered systems. Start with Orion: NASA documents both how its cabin works and what engineers learned when its heat shield returned from Artemis I.

01

A cabin is a system that keeps working.

Orion’s environmental control and life support systems, explained from NASA’s Crew Systems reference.

Four connected jobs inside Orion
JobHow NASA describes it
Manage the atmosphereSupply oxygen, remove carbon dioxide and trace contaminants, ventilate the cabin, and filter particles and microbes.
Control pressureHigh-pressure oxygen and nitrogen tanks supply the crew environment. The system can also be operated manually.
Supply waterStore and distribute potable water for drinking, food preparation, hygiene and medical needs.
Monitor the environmentTrack temperature, humidity and pressure to detect unsafe cabin conditions.

Source: NASA — Crew Systems, “Environmental Control and Life Support Systems.” These details describe Orion; other spacecraft use different implementations.

02

Clean the air. Regenerate. Repeat.

Follow Orion’s regenerable carbon-dioxide and humidity removal process.

  1. Cabin exposure. The removal system takes up carbon dioxide and humidity from cabin air.
  2. Vacuum exposure. Exposure to space vents the collected carbon dioxide and humidity overboard.
  3. Return to service. Regeneration prepares the system to clean cabin air again.
Why regenerate instead of carry more consumables?

NASA identifies mass and volume savings as a reason for Orion’s regenerable system, contrasting it with expendable carbon-dioxide-removal chemicals used on the Space Shuttle. Regeneration reduces the need to carry replacement removal material for that task.

Source: NASA — Crew Systems. This is a conceptual sequence, not a plumbing schematic or a simulation of valve timing.

03

The heat shield came back with a question.

A documented engineering investigation: Artemis I’s unexpected Avcoat char loss.

NASA’s December 2024 investigation reported that gases generated inside Orion’s ablative Avcoat could not escape as expected. Pressure built up, cracking the material and releasing charred pieces.

The entry environment mattered. Between the atmospheric dips of the skip-entry trajectory, heating decreased while energy accumulated inside the material. Ground tests conducted before flight at higher heating rates had allowed permeable char to form and gases to escape; they had not reproduced the same cracking behaviour.

Post-flight testing reproduced the relevant environment. NASA reported that locally permeable areas of the flown shield had avoided the cracking and char loss. This case shows why reproducing the flight conditions matters alongside choosing a test temperature.

What this case does—and does not—establish

These are NASA’s published findings for the uncrewed Artemis I flight, not a universal description of all ablative shields. The article reports cabin-system temperatures within limits. It does not justify treating every pattern of material loss as expected, or assigning all spacecraft one re-entry temperature.

Source: NASA — NASA Identifies Cause of Artemis I Orion Heat Shield Char Loss, 5 December 2024. Historical case study; not a statement of current flight readiness.
