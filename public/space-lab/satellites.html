Satellites

A satellite is just an object that's falling around something else instead of into it. Which orbit you put one in (how high, how fast, how tilted) depends entirely on what you need it to actually do.

The geostationary orbit wasn't discovered by an engineer. It was proposed by a science fiction writer. In a 1945 magazine article, Arthur C. Clarke worked out that a satellite at the right altitude above the equator would orbit at exactly the speed the Earth rotates, appearing to hang motionless in the sky. Communications satellites still use that orbit today.

Every satellite orbit is a trade-off between altitude, speed, and coverage, and engineers group the useful ones into three broad families. Low Earth orbit, roughly 160 to 2,000 km up, is where the ISS, most Earth-observation satellites, and nearly every CubeSat fly: close enough for sharp imagery and short communication delays, but moving fast enough that a single satellite only sees any given point on the ground for a few minutes at a time.

Medium Earth orbit, a band centered around 20,000 km, is where GPS and similar navigation constellations live, high enough that a modest number of satellites can cover the whole planet between them. Geostationary orbit sits much higher still, at exactly the altitude where a satellite's orbital period matches Earth's rotation: 35,786 km. A satellite there appears fixed in the sky from the ground, which is exactly why geostationary orbit is where most television and weather satellites are parked: a ground antenna can point at one spot and never have to move.

What all three orbits share is physics, not engineering choice: how fast a satellite has to move to stay up, and how long it takes to go around, are both determined entirely by altitude. Lower orbits move faster and circle the planet quickly; higher orbits move slower and take longer.
