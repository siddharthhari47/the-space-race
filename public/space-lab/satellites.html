Satellites

A satellite is an object falling around the Earth instead of into it. Where you put it decides what it can see, how often it can see it, and who on the ground is ever allowed to talk to it.

01

Where it actually goes

The orbit stays put; the planet turns underneath it. That one fact explains the whole shape below — the sine wave, the westward march, and why geostationary is a dot.

Take the inclination to zero and watch the track flatten onto the equator. Take it to 90° and it sweeps the poles. The highest latitude a satellite ever reaches is exactly its inclination — so anything shaded above the dashed line is territory that orbit simply never flies over, no matter how long you wait.

Set it to the geostationary preset and the track collapses to a single point. Nothing in the code special-cases that; it falls out of the arithmetic, because at 35,786 km the orbital period matches one rotation of the Earth and the two motions cancel exactly.

02

The orbit that flies backwards on purpose

Press Sun-sync above and look at the inclination: it is over 90°, which means the satellite is going round the wrong way. That is deliberate, and it is one of the neatest tricks in orbital mechanics.

Earth is not a sphere. It bulges at the equator, and that extra mass tugs at any orbit that is not exactly equatorial, slowly dragging the whole orbital plane around. For most missions this is a nuisance to be corrected.

Unless you choose an inclination that makes the drift come out at exactly one revolution per year. Then the orbit plane keeps a fixed angle to the Sun, and the satellite crosses every latitude at the same local time on every single pass — so today's image and last month's image have identical lighting and identical shadows. The nuisance becomes the instrument.

Solving that condition gives a negative cosine, which means an inclination above 90°. Every sun-synchronous satellite in existence is flying slightly retrograde because that is the only way the sum works out.

03

Why the launch site decides the orbit

Drag the inclination below 28.5° and watch Cape Canaveral drop off the list of places you could have launched from.

An orbital plane has to pass through the point you launched from. A plane through a site at latitude L is tilted at least L from the equator — so you cannot reach an inclination lower than your own latitude, not without a plane change, which is among the most expensive manoeuvres in spaceflight. Launching east also collects free speed from Earth's rotation, and that bonus is largest at the equator.

This is the real reason the ISS sits at 51.6°. It is not an optimum for science or coverage — it is the lowest inclination reachable from Baikonur, and the station had to be somewhere Soyuz could get to.

Geostationary orbit was not proposed by an engineer. In a 1945 magazine article, the science fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke worked out that a satellite at the right altitude above the equator would circle at exactly the rate Earth turns, and appear to hang motionless. Television and weather satellites still park there.
