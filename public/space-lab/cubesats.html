CubeSats

A CubeSat is defined entirely by its size, not its mission. That standardization, more than any single invention, is what let the modern Space Race spread far beyond a handful of national space agencies.

The CubeSat standard was co-created by Bob Twiggs at Stanford and Jordi Puig-Suari at Cal Poly, alongside Cal Poly's P-POD deployer, specifically so university students could build and fly real spacecraft instead of only simulating them on paper.

One unit, 1U, is a 10cm cube weighing around 1.33kg; missions needing more room simply add units — 2U, 3U, 6U, 12U, and beyond. The standard was created in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari at California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs at Stanford, alongside Cal Poly's P-POD deployer, a spring-loaded tube that ejects CubeSats into orbit once their rocket arrives.

Building to that standard is dramatically cheaper than designing a satellite from scratch, and that's what opened the door. Universities, startups, and small national space programmes — several flying their first ever satellite as a CubeSat with help from international partnerships like Japan's BIRDS Project — have all used the same standard to reach orbit for a fraction of what a traditional satellite costs.

Exploded View

A CubeSat looks deceptively simple from the outside: a metal box roughly the size of a Rubik's Cube. Open it up and every cubic centimeter is fought over: solar panels, batteries, a radio, a flight computer, sometimes an entire scientific instrument, all stacked into less room than a shoebox. Pull it apart layer by layer and see what actually fits.

Build a CubeSat

Every real CubeSat mission starts with the same fight: too many components, not enough volume or mass. Pick a size, add the components your mission actually needs, and see how fast the budget runs out. Most first attempts don't fit, and that's completely normal.

If yours didn't fit either, you've just run into the same wall every real CubeSat team hits early. The fix is almost never a bigger battery. It's usually cutting an instrument.

Power Budget

Every CubeSat has to generate at least as much power as it uses, averaged over an orbit that includes roughly a third spent in Earth's shadow, with the solar panels producing nothing at all.

A budget that just barely balances on paper often fails in orbit. Batteries degrade, panels get shadowed by the spacecraft's own structure, and margin that looked comfortable in a spreadsheet evaporates fast.

Orbit Simulator

Altitude decides almost everything about a CubeSat's life: how fast it moves, how long it survives before atmospheric drag drags it back down, and how often it passes over a ground station that can actually hear it. Adjust altitude and inclination and watch the orbit — and its period — change in real time.

Notice how a CubeSat at low altitudes only stays up a few years before drag brings it down. That's not a flaw. It's a feature engineers actually rely on: dead satellites eventually clear themselves out of orbit instead of piling up as debris forever.

More Tools

Two pieces of the CubeSat picture aren't built yet: a mission planner, for laying out an orbit, a payload, and a ground station schedule together the way a real mission designer has to juggle all three at once, and a communications simulator, showing how link budget, ground station count, and data rate trade off once your CubeSat is actually flying and trying to phone home. Both are in development.

Going Further

CubeSats have gone well past Earth orbit. In May 2018, two 6U CubeSats, MarCO-A and MarCO-B, launched alongside NASA's InSight lander and rode along to Mars, relaying its signal home during entry, descent, and landing — the first time a CubeSat had survived deep space. Not every CubeSat is chasing a deep-space first, though. Companies like Planet Labs run entire fleets of small CubeSats that photograph the whole of Earth's landmass daily — work that used to require a handful of far larger, far more expensive satellites.
