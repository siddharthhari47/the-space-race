Planetary Missions

Everything else in Space Lab stays close to Earth. This is what happens when a mission leaves orbit behind entirely and heads for somewhere else.

Voyager 1 is the most distant human-made object in existence, now well over 24 billion kilometers from Earth and still transmitting — a radio signal that takes over 22 hours to reach us, from a spacecraft launched in 1977 on hardware far less capable than a modern calculator.

A planetary mission has to solve problems Earth-orbit missions never face: years or decades in transit, one-shot maneuvers with no possibility of a do-over, and communication delays long enough that nothing can be flown by joystick in real time. Every decision has to be made in advance or handled autonomously by the spacecraft itself.

Voyager 1 and 2 launched in 1977 to take advantage of a rare planetary alignment that let a single spacecraft visit multiple outer planets using gravity assists — a launch window that wouldn't recur for another 175 years. Both spacecraft completed their planetary flybys decades ago and have since left the solar system's planetary region entirely, and both are still returning data today, running on systems designed in the 1970s.

Landing on another world is a different kind of hard. Sojourner, which reached Mars in 1997 aboard the Pathfinder mission, was the first wheeled rover to operate there — a microwave-oven-sized vehicle that proved a rover could survive the trip and actually drive once it arrived, opening the door to every larger, more capable Mars rover that followed.

Ingenuity took that a step further in 2021, becoming the first aircraft to achieve powered, controlled flight on another planet. Mars's atmosphere is barely one percent the density of Earth's, which meant Ingenuity's rotors had to spin far faster than any helicopter's on Earth just to generate enough lift — a problem that had never needed solving before, because nothing had ever tried to fly there.
