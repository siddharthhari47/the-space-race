Space Stations

A spacecraft gets someone to orbit. A space station is the much harder promise that followed: that people could actually live and work up there, not just visit.

The International Space Station is one of the most expensive objects ever built, assembled from pieces launched by multiple countries over more than a decade. Exact lifetime cost figures vary depending on what's counted, but by any measure it represents one of the largest sustained engineering investments humanity has made in a single structure.

The Soviet Union launched Salyut 1, the first space station, in 1971 — a single module with no ability to be resupplied or expanded, designed to prove that people could live in orbit for weeks at a time rather than days. It succeeded at that, though its first crew tragically died during their return to Earth, a reminder of how unforgiving the engineering margins were in that era.

Stations after Salyut grew more ambitious: Skylab in the United States, then Mir, which the Soviet Union and later Russia operated for fifteen years and which pioneered the idea of a modular station built up piece by piece over time rather than launched as one unit. The International Space Station, assembled starting in 1998, took that modular approach further than any station before it, built from components launched by multiple countries and space agencies over more than a decade.

Since 2000, the ISS has never been empty. Crews rotate in and out every few months, but there has been at least one person aboard continuously for close to a quarter century — by far the longest unbroken human presence off the surface of the Earth, and the clearest proof yet that long-duration life in orbit is genuinely sustainable, not just a demonstration.
