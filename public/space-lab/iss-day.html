A day on the ISS

Three things about a day on the International Space Station surprise people. The crew float, but gravity is still pulling on them. They see about sixteen sunrises before bedtime. And you can look up and see the station from your garden, but only at certain times of night. Guess each one, then find out why.

Level 1: why they float

Throw something sideways from the station's height and see where it goes. The speed you set is the craft's speed round the Earth's centre, not a speed relative to the station. The Earth is drawn to true scale, so the thin dashed ring just above the ground is this exhibit's own line for “the air gets thick here” (120 km): a throw that gets that low stops. In the box, an astronaut and an apple float. Watch what the two do on every throw, including the ones that fall in.

Level 2: sixteen sunrises

You are looking down on the orbit from above. The Sun is on the left and the Earth throws a shadow to the right. The ring around it is a 24 hour clock on GMT, which is the time the crew keep, with the crew's day marked on it. Press Play or drag the Time slider, and tap Sunrise! each time the station comes out of the shadow.

The real angle swings between −75° and +75° over the year; for about 36 days of the year 2014 it was over 60° (NASA thermal analysis course). Changing it restarts the count.

    The ring shows one day from a NASA crew timeline and the 2130 to 0600 GMT sleep window NASA gives; exercise is two hours a day but is spread through the working day on each person's own schedule, so it is not a block. Times change day to day.

    Level 3: spot it from home

    Tap the map to stand somewhere, or use the two sliders. A pass counts as visible when all three hold: the station is at least 10° above the horizon (the exhibit's own rule), it is in sunlight, and the Sun is at least 6° below your horizon. Find a visible pass and watch it cross the sky.

    To see it for real, NASA's Spot the Station lists the passes over your town.

    When you can see it

    Falling and floating are the same thing here. A thrown ball falls toward the ground and, if it is thrown fast enough sideways, the ground curves away under it as fast as it falls. At the station's height that speed is about 7.7 km/s in the model, and the station, the crew and the apple all do it together, so none of them presses on any of the others. That is why the apple in the box never moves away from the astronaut, even on the throws that fall into the thick air.

    Sixteen sunrises are just sixteen laps. One lap takes about 92 minutes in the model, and each lap goes once through the Earth's shadow and once out of it. The shadow's length depends on the angle between the Sun and the orbit's plane, which changes through the year; at a large enough angle the station never enters it.

    The station is only a point of reflected sunlight. In the middle of the day the sky is too bright to see it; later in the night it passes into the Earth's shadow and nothing is lighting it (in the model, once the Sun is about 20° below the horizon of a spot the station is overhead). In between, a while after sunset or before sunrise, the sky at your spot is dark while the station, 400 km up, is still in the Sun. That narrow window is the only one, and which days have a pass in it depends on where the orbit's plane sits against the Sun.

    Model values are worked out on this page and are not measurements of any day's orbit. The model's height is the exhibit's choice inside NASA's range of 370 to 460 km.

    What the model leaves out: the Earth's bulge (except for the slow turning of the orbit used on “next day”), the air, the Moon and Sun's pull, and the station's real shape and attitude. Light is treated as parallel rays, so the shadow has a sharp edge, with no soft edge and no bending by the air. The Sun is held still for the day and, on level 3, placed on the celestial equator, as on an equinox. The 120 km stop line, the 10° minimum height and the catch window in the tap game are this exhibit's own rules, not NASA's. Sightings in the real world also depend on cloud, haze, the station's changing brightness and the local horizon.

    The crew float because they are falling, they see a sunrise every orbit, and you see the station only when your sky is dark and it is still in the Sun.
