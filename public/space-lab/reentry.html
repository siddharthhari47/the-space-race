Bring it home

A capsule coming back from the Moon meets the air at about 11 kilometres a second. It has one narrow range of angles to get through: too shallow and it bounces back out, too steep and the slowing goes over the 12 g line this exhibit uses for “too much”.

The capsule has no steering: a ballistic Apollo command module, as it was when it reached the air at 400,000 feet. Set the angle below the horizon and fire. Each try leaves a mark on the strip under the picture, and the edges of the safe range show up as the marks change shape.

Level 2: roll the lift

The real capsule made a little lift as well as drag (a lift-to-drag ratio of about 0.3), and the crew could point that lift up, down or sideways by rolling the capsule. Up holds it out of the thick air longer and cuts the load; down pulls it into the air sooner. Pick one roll for the whole entry. In this model a little lift down rescues only a slightly shallower entry, and full lift down crushes it, so the big gain is on the steep side.

The width of the safe window

Where lift helps most

The slowing a capsule feels is the drag of the air divided by its weight, counted in g. A steep entry reaches thick air while it is still very fast, so the drag builds up quickly and peaks high. A shallow one skims thin air for a long time. Too shallow and the capsule never gets deep enough into the air to shed its speed before it climbs out again. In the model a no-lift capsule that only just stays in does not settle at once: it dips, climbs part way out, and comes down again in a second and harder dip, so just steeper than the skip edge the peak load is a little higher than a few tenths of a degree further in.

Lift widens the window, but mostly at one end. Rolled up, the lift pushes the capsule away from the Earth, so a steeper entry no longer piles on the load; in the model that gains well over a degree. Rolled down a little, it pulls the capsule into the air, so a slightly shallower entry no longer skips, but the gain is only a fraction of a degree and a full roll down piles on the load instead. No single roll does both, so the wide window needs the roll chosen to suit the angle. The real Apollo guidance changed the roll during the entry; this exhibit holds one roll for the whole entry, which is a simplification of its own.

The real corridor is bounded by landing-range limits as well as by the load, and it is drawn for a range of capsule and atmosphere conditions, so it is not the same thing as the model's window and the two should not be expected to match.

What the model leaves out: the Earth turns, and the capsule's sideways lift goes to steering it across the ground, not just up and down. The air is a standard day's, the same every time. The capsule is a point with a fixed drag and a fixed lift. Heating is a standard formula for the convective heating of the hottest spot on the heat shield, not a heat-shield model, and it leaves out the radiative heating that adds to it at lunar-return speed. The 12 g limit is the exhibit's own rule for "over": it is the load the Apollo entry planners drew their steep boundary at, and it is not a limit on what a person can survive.

The way home from the Moon is a degree or two wide, and lift is what lets a capsule use more of it.
