Interlude

Saturn V

One hundred and ten metres, three stages, and 33,360,000 newtons of thrust at liftoff. The rest of this page is what it did.

  1. First stage · S‑IC

    Five F‑1 engines, 6,672,000 newtons of thrust each and later uprated to 6,805,000. They burned kerosene and liquid oxygen for two and a half minutes and were finished by about 61 kilometres.

  2. Separation

    The black ring is the interstage. Staging ran on small solid rockets: eight retro motors at 391,000 newtons to drag the spent stage clear, and eight ullage motors at 101,000 newtons each to shove the vehicle forward first, so the propellant sat at the bottom of the tank where the pumps could find it.

  3. Launch escape system

    Three solid motors on a lattice above the command module: an escape motor at 667,000 newtons, a pitch motor at 13,300, and a jettison motor at 178,000 whose only job was to throw the whole assembly away once it was no longer wanted.

  4. Second stage · S‑II

    Five J‑2 engines burning liquid hydrogen, 889,600 newtons each and later 1,023,000. Six minutes took the vehicle to 184 kilometres and 24,620 km/h — nearly orbit, and not quite.

  5. Third stage · S‑IVB

    One J‑2, and the only engine on the vehicle that had to start twice. Two and three quarter minutes put the spacecraft in a 190‑kilometre orbit; two hours and 44 minutes after liftoff it lit again and pushed it to 39,400 km/h, out of Earth orbit altogether.

Figures from Roger E. Bilstein, Stages to Saturn, NASA SP‑4206 — page 5 and the Appendix A schematic. The height is measured off NASA's own model of the vehicle, not taken from the book. Where these come from.

About me

The short version is below. The long one is on its own page.

Hi, I'm Sid.

I'm an Electronics and Communication Engineering student at BITS Pilani, and I'm a little obsessed with figuring out how things work.

It started at a Boeing plant, sometime in middle school. I don't think I understood the scale of what I was looking at. I just remember that the building was absurd, the aircraft inside it were somehow more absurd, and I did not shut up about it for weeks afterwards. My dad bought me a model 747-8 on that trip, and it did not stay a model for very long. I started modifying it with clay — solar panels along the top of the wings, because the sun was right there, so why wouldn't you use it — and I had an entire list of improvements that Boeing had apparently overlooked.

Nobody had explained mass budgets to me, or drag, or what actually happens to a control surface when you press a slab of clay onto it. I understand a lot more about why most of those ideas wouldn't work now. The instinct hasn't changed, though. I still look at a machine and immediately start wondering what I'd do differently. The only real difference is that now I have to check.

Which is roughly what this site is for. Learning aerospace mostly means reading, and then more reading, until you have seventeen tabs open and a headache. I wanted to find out whether you could interact with the thing instead — rotate the aircraft, change the orbit, build the CubeSat, and see what breaks. It's part learning resource, part experiment, and part me seeing how far a browser will go.

If you leave here knowing every equation behind orbital mechanics, I've probably failed a little. I'd rather you left with a question.

Interlude

Descent

Three days of coasting, then twelve and a half minutes of flying. The times below are ground elapsed time — hours, minutes and seconds since the clock started at 13:32:00 UTC on 16 July.

  1. 002:50:13 · Translunar injection

    The S‑IVB shut down and the spacecraft was on its way. What followed was seventy‑three hours of coasting on a curve already decided, which is most of the mission and almost none of the story.

  2. 075:49:50 · Lunar orbit insertion

    A retrograde burn of five minutes and 58 seconds, made at closest approach and therefore on the far side — where the Moon itself is between the spacecraft and every antenna on Earth. Nobody in Houston could have helped, and nobody in Houston knew it had worked until the signal came back.

  3. 100:12:00 · Undocking

    Eagle undocked from Columbia and Armstrong rotated it so Collins could look it over and check that all four landing legs were out. An hour and 24 minutes later it made a 30‑second burn to drop the low point of its orbit toward the surface. Collins stayed with the command module. He is the reason there are photographs of Eagle in lunar orbit.

  4. 102:33:05 · Powered descent

    Twelve minutes and 36 seconds, engine lit the whole way. Five times in the last seven and a half minutes the guidance computer stopped to report that it had more work queued than it could finish — four 1202s and a 1201 — and five times it shed the low priority work and kept flying. It was designed to do that. The people who had to decide, in seconds, that it was designed to do that were about 384,000 kilometres away.

  5. 102:45:40 · Contact

    The engine was blowing dust across the ground from about 100 feet up, and the lower they came the thicker it got. The fuel low‑level light had come on about 70 seconds before the footpads touched.

The times in this act are from Richard W. Orloff, Apollo by the Numbers: A Statistical Reference, NASA SP‑4029, checked against NASA’s Apollo 11 Mission Report (MSC‑00171) and the Apollo 11 Flight Journal and Lunar Surface Journal. Where these come from.
