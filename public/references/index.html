The Earth behind the homepage is a live render, not a photograph. The surface, cloud and night-lights imagery is NASA's and is credited below; the camera moves, the atmospheric scattering and the vehicle are this site's own work. Nothing on that layer is a NASA photograph, and nothing on it should be read as one. Where it uses real numbers — the pad's coordinates, the launch time, the position of the Sun that morning — those numbers come from the sources listed below and are checked by a test harness in the repository rather than eyeballed. A Saturn V is 110.6 m tall and the Earth's radius is 6,371 km, a ratio of about 58 thousand to one. At any framing that shows the curve of the planet the vehicle is smaller than one pixel, so both its size and its altitude are drawn up. Every visualisation of a launch from orbit does this; this one says by how much. The numbers, and everything else on that layer that is invented, are set out under The Apollo 11 Launch below. What is not exaggerated: the order of events, the times they happen at, and the distance the vehicle travels downrange. At the opening framing the camera is 3,150 km up, and one pixel of a 1600-wide frame covers about 2.9 km of ground. A Saturn V is four hundredths of that pixel; its exhaust plume, tens of metres across, is not much better off. Neither would be visible from there in daylight however good the render was. So the warm point of light on the surface is invented. It marks where the vehicle is standing and when something is happening there, at a size the eye can find. The brightness is not a measurement of anything. The planet behind the homepage goes round in about thirteen minutes: 0.45 degrees a second against the real 0.0042, roughly a hundred and eight times too fast. At the true rate nothing would move within any visit anyone is going to make and the Earth would read as a photograph. The direction and the axis are right, and the Sun holds still where it was at 13:32 GMT on 16 July 1969 while the surface turns through it, which is what a day actually looks like from up there. The speed is the part that has been made up.

The ascent on the homepage is a real sequence of real events at real times, drawn at sizes that are not real at all. Everything that has been exaggerated is listed here, with the number, because an exaggeration nobody is told about is just a mistake.

The pad and the clock. Launch Complex 39A's geodetic coordinates (28.608422° N, 80.604133° W) and the launch time (Range Zero 13:32:00 GMT on 16 July 1969, actual liftoff 13:32:00.63) are taken from the Launch Information table and the Apollo 11 narrative in NASA SP-2000-4029, Richard W. Orloff, Apollo by the Numbers: A Statistical Reference (PDF, NASA History Division). They are transcribed at full published precision rather than rounded, because the launch point on the globe is computed from them.

Where the Sun was. The terminator on the homepage is not decorative. The sub-solar point is computed from the low-precision solar position algorithm published by the U.S. Naval Observatory's Astronomical Applications Department as Computing Approximate Solar Coordinates — the Astronomical Almanac's own page-C5 algorithm with its constants refitted against JPL's DE405 ephemeris, quoted as accurate to about one arcminute within two centuries of 2000. The Earth's rotation under that Sun uses the IAU 1982 Greenwich mean sidereal time coefficients as given by Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed., Willmann-Bell, 1998, equation 12.4. Run against the launch epoch the two together put the Sun overhead at 21.342° N, 21.509° W and 36.46° above the horizon at the pad, which is a morning Sun a third of the way up the sky — and which matches the shadows in the launch photography. That check runs in tools/apollo-mission-check.mjs on every build.

Where the Moon was. Not computed. The Sun above is, because a two-term equation of centre is good to an arcminute and fits in a paragraph; the Moon is not like that, and a truncated lunar series written from memory is exactly the kind of thing that reads as authoritative and is quietly a degree wrong. So the Moon's position is measured: geocentric right ascension, declination and range read out of NASA JPL's Horizons system on the DE441 ephemeris, target Moon (301), observer geocentric (500@399), at twelve-hour steps from 1969-07-16 12:00 to 1969-07-21 12:00 UTC. Eleven rows, transcribed into src/apollo/mission.js unchanged — which is the only thing that makes a citation worth anything, since anyone can run the same query and compare. Between samples the site interpolates linearly, which at twelve-hour spacing is good to a few arcminutes against a disc half a degree wide, and the code says so.

Three things fall out of those numbers that are worth stating, because each of them is a fact about the mission rather than a fact about the website. Apollo 11 left with the Moon near apogee: 403,919 km at the launch epoch, closing to 395,550 km by the bottom of the page, so the disc grows from 0.493° to 0.503° — not the 384,400 km everyone quotes. The Moon was 22.2° from the Sun, which makes it about 1.8 days old: a thin waxing crescent from Earth. And that crescent is the launch window. Tranquility Base had to be near its own sunrise so a low Sun threw long shadows off the boulders and Armstrong could read the ground he was setting down on; wanting sunrise there on the 20th is what fixed a launch on the 16th.

Which way is lunar north. The Moon's rotation axis is neither the Earth's nor the normal to its own orbit. It sits about 1.5° from the pole of the ecliptic (Britannica puts the tilt at 1°32′), and unlike most things about the Moon that angle stays put, so this site takes lunar north to be the ecliptic pole exactly — right ascension 270°, declination 90° minus the obliquity, 23.4393° at J2000 — and drops the degree and a half. The Moon is drawn tidally locked, near side to the Earth, using the orientation convention recovered in tools/build-moon-texture.py and confirmed there against Tycho and Mare Crisium. Libration is not modelled: the real Moon rocks about eight degrees in longitude and seven in latitude over a month, which is how we see about 59% of the surface instead of half of it (NASA SVS, Moon Wobble).

Checking a transcription. Transcribed numbers cannot be checked against the formula that produced them, because there isn't one. So tools/apollo-mission-check.mjs checks them against physics instead. The Sun lies in the plane of the ecliptic by definition, so the angle from the ecliptic pole to the Sun must be exactly 90°; it comes out at 89.997°, and that single number requires the obliquity, the right-ascension convention, the sidereal-time formula and the latitude-longitude-to-vector conversion to all be right together. The lunar orbit is inclined 5.145° to the ecliptic, a hard bound with nothing to do with the Sun; the Moon here never strays more than 3.07°. And the Moon must cross the sky at about 13 degrees a day rather than 360, which is what separates reading it in a frame frozen at the launch epoch — correct, since the Earth turns underneath the Moon rather than the reverse — from reading it against a live sidereal time, which would whirl it round the planet once a day and pass every other check on this page.

Why the Moon is not a shaded ball. The full Moon is not brightest in the middle and dimmer at the edges; it is close to flat, bright right out to the limb, and it is much brighter at full than the lit fraction alone would suggest. That is lunar regolith: a deep, porous layer whose shadows hide behind the grains casting them as the phase angle closes, which astronomers call the opposition effect. So the Moon here is shaded with a Lommel-Seeliger term — brightness going as μ0 / (μ0 + μ), the single-scattering law for a semi-infinite particulate surface and the standard base for lunar photometry — blended with a little Lambert in the manner of the "lunar-Lambert" correction used on spacecraft imagery. The blend is tuned by eye, not fitted, and the code says that too. Earthshine on the dark limb is real and is drawn far brighter than it is: it is far fainter than direct sunlight, which on a screen is black.

Close to the lunar surface. Desktop crater shading comes from the global LOLA elevation map. Fine grain and small surface undulations are illustrative, not a reconstruction of Tranquility Base. The lander's ground shadow is an approximate body projection along the scene's sunlight. Exhaust brightness, the radial dust sheet, and their fade curves are illustrative. They follow the mission clock when scrolling in either direction; the visible engine fade finishes at contact because that is where this page's clock stops, rather than depicting the later engine cutoff.

The commentary. The count in the top right of the homepage quotes Jack King, the launch Public Affairs Officer, from NASA's own transcript Apollo 11 Highlights, Day 1 (PDF), which runs the PAO, CAPCOM and spacecraft loops for the first day of the mission. Four complete sentences are lifted from it unedited: Astronauts report it feels good., Guidance is internal., Ignition sequence starts. and All engines running. Liftoff! We have a liftoff, 32 minutes past the hour, liftoff on Apollo 11. Tower cleared. Two things about that wording are worth stating plainly, because both look like mistakes and neither is. The transcript reads All engines running and Ignition sequence starts, where the line is far more often quoted as all engine running and ignition sequence start; NASA's document is what is reproduced here. And King's spoken count really does skip eight and seven — it reads 12, 11, 10, 9. Ignition sequence starts. 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0. — because he filled those two seconds with the call. The clock still passes through 8 and 7, since it is a clock, but no caption is attached to them.

What the count is not. It opens at T−60 seconds. The real terminal count ran for hours and the full countdown for days; sixty seconds is chosen because it is where the commentary everyone knows begins, and because the whole of it then fits inside the first screen of scroll. It is an authored figure in the same way the drawn scale of the vehicle is, and like that one it is listed here rather than left to be discovered. The count advances only with scroll position — it is a reading of how far down the page you are, not a timer, so it holds still if you do.

Numerical source: NASA TMR's CL/CD data extract, 80 grit zone only. Conditions in the file: NACA 0012, Reynolds number 6 million, Mach 0.15, transition tripped; zone surface treatment: 80 grit.
