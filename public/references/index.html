Every factual page on this site points back here. Some of these sections have real sources in them already; a lot are still empty, because "coming soon" is more honest than a made-up citation. This page grows as the rest of the site does.

The Earth behind the homepage is a live render, not a photograph. The surface, cloud and night-lights imagery is NASA's and is credited below; the camera moves, the atmospheric scattering and the vehicle are this site's own work. Nothing on that layer is a NASA photograph, and nothing on it should be read as one. Where it uses real numbers — the pad's coordinates, the launch time, the position of the Sun that morning — those numbers come from the sources listed below and are checked by a test harness in the repository rather than eyeballed. A Saturn V is 110.6 m tall and the Earth's radius is 6,371 km, a ratio of about 58 million to one. At any framing that shows the curve of the planet the vehicle is smaller than one pixel, so its apparent size on the homepage is deliberately exaggerated. Every visualisation of a launch from orbit does this; this one says so. Altitudes, the shape of the trajectory and the order of events are not exaggerated. At the opening framing the camera is 3,150 km up, and one pixel of a 1600-wide frame covers about 2.9 km of ground. A Saturn V is four hundredths of that pixel; its exhaust plume, tens of metres across, is not much better off. Neither would be visible from there in daylight however good the render was. So the warm point of light on Merritt Island is invented. It marks where Launch Complex 39A is and when something is happening on it, at a size the eye can find. The position is real and comes from the coordinates cited below. The brightness is not a measurement of anything. The planet behind the homepage goes round in about thirteen minutes: 0.45 degrees a second against the real 0.0042, roughly a hundred and eight times too fast. At the true rate nothing would move within any visit anyone is going to make and the Earth would read as a photograph. The direction and the axis are right, and the Sun holds still where it was at 13:32 GMT on 16 July 1969 while the surface turns through it, which is what a day actually looks like from up there. The speed is the part that has been made up.

The three textures wrapped around the homepage's Earth. Each was downloaded from NASA Visible Earth and then resized and re-encoded to WebP for the web — the geometry and the colours are NASA's, the file format and resolution are not. The exact source files, with their byte counts, are recorded in tools/build-earth-textures.py in this site's repository, which is how each shipped texture can be matched back to the URL it came from.

NASA Visible Earth image record 73751, "Blue Marble: Next Generation with Topography and Bathymetry" — the July 2004 monthly composite, from MODIS on Terra. This is the daylit surface: land, ice, and ocean floor. NASA Visible Earth image record 57747. A single global cloud composite, drifting slowly over the surface layer. It is one snapshot of weather, not the weather of 16 July 1969 — that record does not exist at this resolution. NASA Visible Earth image record 79765, from the Suomi NPP satellite's VIIRS day/night band. Used only on the unlit side of the terminator. It is 2012 lighting shown under 1969 sunlight, which is an anachronism the page accepts in exchange for a night side that is not simply black.

The pad and the clock. Launch Complex 39A's geodetic coordinates (28.608422° N, 80.604133° W) and the launch time (Range Zero 13:32:00 GMT on 16 July 1969, actual liftoff 13:32:00.63) are taken from the Launch Information table and the Apollo 11 narrative in NASA SP-2000-4029, Richard W. Orloff, Apollo by the Numbers: A Statistical Reference (PDF, NASA History Division). They are transcribed at full published precision rather than rounded, because the launch point on the globe is computed from them.

Where the Sun was. The terminator on the homepage is not decorative. The sub-solar point is computed from the low-precision solar position algorithm published by the U.S. Naval Observatory's Astronomical Applications Department as Computing Approximate Solar Coordinates — the Astronomical Almanac's own page-C5 algorithm with its constants refitted against JPL's DE405 ephemeris, quoted as accurate to about one arcminute within two centuries of 2000. The Earth's rotation under that Sun uses the IAU 1982 Greenwich mean sidereal time coefficients as given by Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed., Willmann-Bell, 1998, equation 12.4. Run against the launch epoch the two together put the Sun overhead at 21.342° N, 21.509° W and 36.46° above the horizon at the pad, which is a morning Sun a third of the way up the sky — and which matches the shadows in the launch photography. That check runs in tools/apollo-mission-check.mjs on every build.
