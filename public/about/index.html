Hi, I'm Siddharth.

I'm an Electronics and Communication Engineering student at BITS Pilani, and, unsurprisingly, I'm a little obsessed with figuring out how things work.

I've always been drawn to machines that seem slightly ridiculous when you stop and think about them. An aircraft that weighs hundreds of tonnes flying through the air. A satellite that can keep working thousands of kilometres away from Earth. A rocket that throws itself away from the planet at several kilometres per second and somehow manages to come back.

I wanted to understand how all of that actually works.

And that curiosity is what eventually led to The Space Race.

Why I Built This

A lot of the time, learning aerospace means reading.

A lot of reading.

You find a diagram of a rocket, a paragraph explaining an engine, another diagram explaining an orbit, and eventually you have 17 tabs open and a headache.

I wanted to try something different.

What if you could actually interact with the things you're learning about?

Instead of just reading about an aircraft, rotate it and explore its systems.

Instead of memorising what an orbit is, change the parameters and watch it happen.

Instead of reading about a CubeSat, build one.

That's the idea behind this website.

It's part learning resource, part experiment, and part me trying to see how far I can push a browser.

Why Aerospace?

I've always liked engineering because there is something satisfying about taking an idea that exists on paper and turning it into something that actually works.

Aerospace takes that idea to an extreme.

Every system has constraints.

Mass matters.

Power matters.

Heat matters.

Communication matters.

A tiny design decision can affect an entire mission.

And somehow, engineers have to make all of it work thousands of kilometres away from anyone who can fix it.

That is pretty hard not to find interesting.

How This Started

The Space Race didn't start as some huge master plan.

It started with curiosity.

I wanted to learn more about aircraft, satellites, rockets and the engineering behind them, and I kept finding myself building little experiments along the way.

Eventually, I thought:

Why not put all of this in one place?

So I started building.

The website has changed quite a bit since then. What started as a collection of information gradually became a place for interactive models, simulations, experiments and stories.

And I'm still building it.

What I'm Interested In

I'm currently studying Electronics and Communication Engineering, so naturally I tend to look at aerospace from the electronics and systems side too.

I'm particularly interested in:

Avionics

How computers, sensors and control systems keep an aircraft or spacecraft alive.

Embedded Systems

The tiny computers that quietly run much of the hardware around us.

Spacecraft Systems

Power, communications, attitude control, sensing and all the other systems that have to work together.

AI + Aerospace

I'm interested in where machine learning can actually be useful in aerospace, rather than just putting "AI" in front of everything.

Experimental Engineering

Building things, breaking them, figuring out why they broke, and trying again.

Engineering Portfolio

The interests above aren't abstract — here's what they've actually turned into. A laboratory quality system built during a stint at India's National Test House, a manufacturing quotation automation tool built for KGR Fabtek, and a CubeSat flight computer I'm currently building the verification plan for alongside the hardware, not after it. This Website Is Still a Work in Progress

I don't want this to be a finished textbook.

A textbook has an ending.

This doesn't.

There are still simulations I want to build, spacecraft I want to model, missions I want to explain and a frankly unreasonable number of ideas sitting in my notes.

So if something looks unfinished, it probably is.

That's part of the fun.

What I Hope You Get From It

If you leave this website knowing every equation behind orbital mechanics, I've probably failed a little.

I'd rather you leave with a question.

Something like:

"Wait, how does a satellite actually stay up there?"

or

"How does that engine produce that much thrust?"

or

"Could I build one of these?"

Because that's how I ended up here in the first place.

Curiosity comes first. The engineering comes after.
