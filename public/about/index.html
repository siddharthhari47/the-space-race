Why This Site Exists

This started as three blog posts. No plan, no roadmap — just a habit of reading about aerospace and wanting to write down the interesting parts before I forgot them. The Space Race and Where Are We Now went up first, then What About the Aviation, and somewhere in there, writing "the Space Race never ended, it evolved" stopped being a nice line and started being the actual point of the site. Everything since — the labs, the simulators, the 3D aircraft you can rotate and click through — has been in service of that one idea. The competition that put a flag on the Moon in 1969 didn't stop. It just stopped looking like a race between two countries.

How I Got Into Aerospace

I don't have a single moment I can point to — no airshow, no rocket launch watched live, no relative who flew for a living. It's been more of a slow accumulation: reading about how a wing actually generates lift and realizing the popular explanation most people get taught is wrong, learning that the CubeSat standard exists because a couple of professors got tired of watching good student satellite designs die in a lab instead of reaching orbit — that kind of thing. Curiosity that compounds, rather than curiosity that started with a bang.

How the Project Evolved

What began as a place to post the occasional two-minute read about the Space Race turned into something bigger, almost by accident. First it needed real pages instead of a Wix template. Then those pages needed actual interactive tools, not just illustrations, so a CubeSat builder and an orbit simulator showed up. Then a static picture of an aircraft wasn't good enough anymore, so now there's a real 3D scan of a Boeing 777 you can rotate and click through, part by part. Each step happened because the previous one wasn't quite enough — which is probably how most projects like this actually grow, whatever the finished version ends up looking like in hindsight.

What I Hope Visitors Take Away

Mainly, that aerospace engineering is a lot more approachable than it looks from the outside. A wing generating lift, a CubeSat squeezing a whole mission into a shoebox, two spacecraft docking at orbital velocity — none of it requires a physics degree to understand at the level that actually matters. It requires someone willing to explain it without hiding behind jargon, and that's what this site is trying to be.

What I'm Currently Learning

Right now, it's the engineering side more than the history: hydraulics, power systems, communications link budgets — the kind of thing Engineering Lab is going to be built around. This section will keep changing as that changes, so check back.

Where This Is Going

Short term, finishing what's already started: the rest of Engineering Lab, the simulators still marked coming soon, a real 3D model for spacecraft the same way the aircraft explorer got one. Longer term, the hope is that this becomes the site I wish had existed when I first got curious about any of this — something to point a curious teenager toward instead of a Wikipedia rabbit hole or a forty-minute YouTube video.
