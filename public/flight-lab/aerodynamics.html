Aerodynamics

Everything on this site that flies through air rather than vacuum — an airliner, a fighter jet, a rocket in its first few seconds — answers to the same handful of rules. This is where those rules actually come from.

The upturned wingtips on modern airliners aren't styling — they're winglets, and they exist to fight a specific loss. High pressure air under the wing spills around the tip to the low-pressure air on top, rolling into a vortex that drags the aircraft back. A winglet blocks part of that spillage, cutting fuel burn by a few percent for very little added weight.

Four forces act on anything flying through air: lift holding it up, weight pulling it down, thrust pushing it forward, and drag holding it back. Level, unaccelerated flight is just those four forces in balance — change any one of them and the aircraft climbs, descends, speeds up, or slows down.

Lift itself is often explained badly. The popular version — that air splits at the leading edge and has to "catch up" over the longer path across the curved top of the wing — isn't actually true. What really happens is closer to Newton's third law: a wing angled into the airflow deflects air downward and slightly forward, and the air pushes back on the wing in the opposite direction — up. The pressure difference between the wing's upper and lower surfaces, the part Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 work on fluid pressure helps explain, is a real and necessary part of the picture too; the two explanations describe the same phenomenon rather than competing with each other.

How much lift a wing generates depends heavily on its angle of attack — the angle between the wing and the oncoming air, not the angle between the wing and the ground. Increase that angle and lift increases, up to a point. Push past a critical angle, typically somewhere around 15 to 20 degrees depending on the wing's design, and airflow over the top separates from the surface instead of following it. Lift collapses almost immediately. That's a stall, and it's a function of angle of attack alone — it can happen at any speed, in any attitude, nose up or nose down.

Drag comes in more than one form. Some of it is simple friction and shape resistance, the kind that increases with speed. Some of it, induced drag, is a direct side effect of producing lift in the first place, and it's strongest at low speed and high angle of attack — which is exactly why aircraft feel most sluggish and vulnerable right after takeoff and just before landing.
