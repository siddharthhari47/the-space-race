Aerodynamics

Everything on this site that flies through air rather than vacuum — an airliner, a fighter jet, a rocket in its first few seconds — answers to the same handful of rules. This is where those rules actually come from.

The upturned wingtips on modern airliners aren't styling — they're winglets, and they exist to fight a specific loss. High pressure air under the wing spills around the tip to the low-pressure air on top, rolling into a vortex that drags the aircraft back. A winglet blocks part of that spillage, cutting fuel burn by a few percent for very little added weight. See one up close on the Interactive Aircraft Explorer, one of eighteen parts you can click through on a real 777-300ER.

Every aircraft in steady, level flight is holding still in a four-way tug-of-war. Lift pulls up, weight pulls down, thrust pulls forward, drag pulls back, and as long as all four stay balanced, nothing changes. Nudge just one of them and the balance breaks: more thrust than drag and the aircraft speeds up, more lift than weight and it climbs.

Most people get taught that a wing's curved top makes air travel farther, so it speeds up to "catch up" with the air underneath, and faster air means lower pressure, pulling the wing up. It sounds neat. It's also wrong: the air on top doesn't actually arrive at the trailing edge at the same moment as the air below, so there's nothing to catch up to. What actually happens is closer to Newton's third law. A wing angled into the airflow shoves air downward and slightly forward, and the air shoves back, up, exactly as hard. Daniel Bernoulli had already worked out the pressure side of this in 1738, over a century before anyone built a working wing, and that pressure difference and the downward deflection aren't competing explanations. They're the same event, described two different ways.

How much lift a wing generates depends heavily on its angle of attack, the angle between the wing and the oncoming air, not the angle between the wing and the ground. Increase that angle and lift increases, up to a point. Push past a critical angle, typically somewhere around 15 to 20 degrees depending on the wing's design, and airflow over the top separates from the surface instead of following it. Lift collapses almost immediately. That's a stall, and it's a function of angle of attack alone. It can happen at any speed, in any attitude, nose up or nose down.

Not all drag comes from the same place. Push anything through air fast enough and friction and shape resistance start eating energy, worse the faster you go. But some drag is a direct side effect of making lift in the first place: induced drag, and it works backwards from the friction kind, getting worse at low speed and high angle of attack instead of high speed. That's exactly why an aircraft feels most sluggish and vulnerable right after takeoff and just before landing, the two moments it's flying slowest and making the most lift at once.

So next time you're watching a plane climb out after takeoff, see if you can spot which force just won.
