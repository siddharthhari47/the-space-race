02

Why a glider's wings are so long

Switch to Explore freely above and drag the aspect ratio from 3 to 30, watching only the L/D readout. Nothing else on that panel changes it nearly as much.

Making lift has an unavoidable cost. High pressure under the wing spills round the tip into the low pressure on top, rolls into a trailing vortex, and drags the aircraft backwards. That is induced drag, and it scales as Cᵄᵢ = Cₗ² / (π · e · AR) — so a longer, narrower wing of the same area pays less of it.

It also runs backwards from the drag you would expect. Friction drag gets worse the faster you go. Induced drag depends on the square of the lift coefficient, and a wing flying slowly has to run a bigger lift coefficient to hold the aircraft up, so induced drag gets worse the slower you go. NASA Glenn ↗

The upturned wingtips on modern airliners are not styling. NASA Glenn describes the idea behind a winglet as weakening the tip vortex, so the flow across the wing is closer to two-dimensional. Richard Whitcomb of NASA Langley tested and analysed winglets in the mid-1970s, and NASA flight tests on a Boeing 707-type airliner found a 6.5% cut in fuel use. You can see one up close on the 777-300ER model.

Source: NASA Glenn, Winglets and Induced Drag Coefficient ↗.
03

The explanation you were probably taught

Most of us are told that a wing's curved top makes the air travel further, so it has to speed up to "catch up" with the air underneath, and faster air means lower pressure, which pulls the wing up. It is neat, it is memorable, and it is wrong. NASA Glenn says the speed over a lifting wing's top is much higher than the "equal transit" story would give, which means the air over the top gets to the trailing edge earlier. There is nothing to catch up to. NASA Glenn ↗

In Explore freely, set the camber to zero. The section becomes symmetric, with identical path lengths top and bottom, and it still makes lift the moment you give it an angle. Whatever is happening, it is not about the distance the air travels.

What is actually happening is closer to Newton. A wing angled into the airflow shoves air downwards, and the air shoves back, upwards, exactly as hard. Bernoulli's Hydrodynamica of 1738 already tied pressure to speed (Britannica ↗) — and the pressure difference and the downward deflection are not competing explanations. They are one event described two ways.

Every aircraft in steady, level flight is holding still in a four-way tug of war: lift up, weight down, thrust forward, drag back. Nudge one and the balance breaks. More thrust than drag and it accelerates; more lift than weight and it climbs. Next time you watch one climb out after takeoff, see if you can spot which force just won.

04

Getting it wrong was part of getting it right

The people who actually built the first working wings did not have the equal-transit myth to unlearn. They had something arguably harder: almost no data, and instruments that lied to them.

Sir George Cayley spent fifty years building gliders. He kept changing the shape of the wings so the air would flow over them properly, added a tail for stability, and had a boy, whose name nobody recorded, fly one of them. Otto Lilienthal went further and flew himself: sixteen types of glider and as many as two thousand glides in five years, steering by shifting his weight. The Wright brothers built one kite in 1899 and three gliders after it. The 1899 kite, a biplane with a five-foot span, was where they tried wing-warping first — twisting the wingtips to bank the aircraft, the ancestor of the aileron.

Even good data could mislead them. The Wrights' 1901 glider made only about a third of the lift their calculations predicted, and when they fitted a model wing and a flat plate to a wheel on the front of a bicycle, the wing needed an angle to the wind more than three times what Lilienthal's data had predicted. They stopped trusting the published tables and built their own wind tunnel in October 1901.

Sources: Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum and NASA Glenn on Cayley, Lilienthal and the Wrights. Background reading: NPTEL course 101101079, Introduction to Aerospace Engineering — Flight, Prof. Rajkumar S. Pant (IIT Bombay), Lecture 23, "Aerofoils: A Visit to the Past."
