Aerodynamics

A wing is a machine for throwing air downwards. Everything else — the curve, the angle, the span, the stall — is detail about how well it does that, and what it costs.

01

Stall it yourself

Take the angle of attack up slowly. Lift climbs in a straight line, exactly as the theory says it should — and then, somewhere past fourteen degrees, it stops climbing and falls off a cliff. Watch the streamlines when it happens.

The colour of each streamline is its speed. Look at the top surface versus the bottom at any positive angle: the upper flow is genuinely faster, and the gap widens as lift grows. That is the lift — faster flow, lower pressure, and an equal and opposite shove of air downwards behind the wing.

Past the stall the streamlines stop following the surface and the wake goes to pieces. The lift arrow shrinks even though the wing is pointed further up than ever, which is the whole counter-intuitive heart of a stall: it is caused by angle, not by speed. It can happen at any airspeed, in any attitude, nose up or nose down.

02

Why a glider's wings are so long

Go back up and drag the aspect ratio from 3 to 30, watching only the L/D readout. Nothing else on that panel changes it nearly as much.

Making lift has an unavoidable cost. High pressure under the wing spills round the tip into the low pressure on top, rolls into a trailing vortex, and drags the aircraft backwards. That is induced drag, and it scales as Cᵄᵢ = Cₗ² / (π · e · AR) — so a longer, narrower wing of the same area pays less of it.

It also runs backwards from the drag you would expect. Friction drag gets worse the faster you go; induced drag gets worse the slower you go, because a slow wing needs a big lift coefficient to hold the aircraft up. That is precisely why an aeroplane feels most sluggish just after takeoff and just before landing — the two moments it is flying slowest while asking the most of its wing.

The upturned wingtips on modern airliners are not styling. A winglet blocks part of that spillage round the tip, which is nearly equivalent to simply making the wing longer — the fix the induced-drag equation above actually calls for — without the structural penalty a longer wing would bring. A longer span means a longer lever arm for the root bending moment, which means a heavier spar to survive it; a winglet buys most of the aerodynamic benefit while adding only a small amount of mass at the tip. It also sidesteps the hard limit that a longer span can hit directly: many airports size their gates to a maximum wingspan, and a winglet does not count against it. You can see one up close on the 777-300ER model, one of eighteen parts you can click through.

03

The explanation you were probably taught

Most of us are told that a wing's curved top makes the air travel further, so it has to speed up to "catch up" with the air underneath, and faster air means lower pressure, which pulls the wing up. It is neat, it is memorable, and it is wrong. The air going over the top does not arrive at the trailing edge at the same moment as the air underneath — it arrives earlier. There is nothing to catch up to.

Set the camber to zero in the tunnel above. The section becomes symmetric, with identical path lengths top and bottom, and it still makes lift the moment you give it an angle. Whatever is happening, it is not about the distance the air travels.

What is actually happening is closer to Newton. A wing angled into the airflow shoves air downwards, and the air shoves back, upwards, exactly as hard. Bernoulli had the pressure side of it in 1738, more than a century before anyone built a working wing — and the pressure difference and the downward deflection are not competing explanations. They are one event described two ways.

Every aircraft in steady, level flight is holding still in a four-way tug of war: lift up, weight down, thrust forward, drag back. Nudge one and the balance breaks. More thrust than drag and it accelerates; more lift than weight and it climbs. Next time you watch one climb out after takeoff, see if you can spot which force just won.

04

Getting it wrong was part of getting it right

The people who actually built the first working wings did not have the equal-transit myth to unlearn. They had something arguably harder: almost no data, and instruments that lied to them.

Sir George Cayley flew the first gliders carrying a person in 1849, and did it with a cambered, not flat, wing — he had already reasoned his way to the shape from first principles, decades before anyone could measure why it worked. Otto Lilienthal followed with a wing built to flap like a bird's, and the Wright brothers built three gliders of their own, testing wing-warping — twisting the whole wingtip to bank the aircraft, the direct ancestor of the aileron — for the first time in July 1899.

They were also badly misled by their own test equipment. Early wind-tunnel work on thin aerofoils was run at very low speed, and at those conditions the airflow looked attached in the smoke traces while it was actually separating in reality. A generation of designers drew real conclusions from data that was quietly wrong, for reasons that would not become clear until Reynolds number was properly understood.
