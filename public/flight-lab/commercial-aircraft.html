Commercial Aircraft

Commercial aviation rarely gets filed under "the Space Race," but it belongs there. The same era that put satellites in orbit also produced the aircraft that made global travel routine, racing forward on many of the same ideas: lighter materials, more efficient engines, and computer-assisted flight decks.

Airbus exists because of government concern, not one company's ambition. It was founded after ministers from Germany, France, and Britain met in 1967, worried the United States was about to lock up the commercial aviation market entirely.

For most of the 20th century, two American manufacturers, Boeing and McDonnell Douglas, dominated commercial aviation. Europe's most ambitious answer was the Concorde, the first supersonic airliner, but it never became commercially viable and was eventually retired.

The result of that 1967 meeting, after the original engine partner Rolls-Royce withdrew over rising costs, was the A300B: a 250-seat, twin-engine jet powered by an American GE engine, first flown in 1972.

The smaller A310 followed, launched with orders from Swiss Air and Lufthansa, with Korean Air becoming Airbus's first customer outside Europe. Britain rejoined the programme with a loan toward development, taking a 20% stake in Airbus through British Aerospace, alongside France and Germany at 37.9% each and Spain holding the rest.

The A310's real advantage was engineering, not just marketing. It used carbon-fibre reinforced plastic on secondary structures, drag-reducing wingtip devices, and a six-CRT cockpit that replaced analog gauges — the ancestor of the ECAM systems still used today. Put next to a modern narrowbody, the numbers hold up better than you'd expect from a jet designed in the 1970s:

Aircraft Total Capacity Top Speed Range Length
Airbus A310 218 560 mph 5,000 mi 154 ft (47 m)
Boeing 737 MAX 230 521 mph 3,300 mi 116 ft 8 in (35.56 m)

That advantage pushed Boeing to build its own twin-engine widebody, the 767, and started a rivalry that still defines commercial aviation today.
