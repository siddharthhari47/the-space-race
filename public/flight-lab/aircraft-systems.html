Aircraft Systems

A wing explains how an aircraft flies. It doesn't explain how a pilot's small hand movements turn into a control surface moving, or how everyone on board keeps breathing seven miles up. That's what the systems underneath are for.

Almost nothing critical on a commercial aircraft exists in a single copy. Hydraulics, electrical generators, even flight computers are duplicated or triplicated, each one capable of running the aircraft alone. The design philosophy isn't "prevent every failure" — it's "make sure no single failure is ever catastrophic."

On early aircraft, moving the control column pulled a cable that physically moved a control surface — direct, mechanical, and limited by how hard a human could pull. Larger, faster aircraft needed more force than muscle could supply, so hydraulic systems took over: pressurized fluid doing the heavy work, with the pilot's input scaled up rather than applied directly.

Fly-by-wire went a step further, replacing the mechanical linkage itself with electronic signals. The pilot's inputs go to a flight computer, which decides how to move the control surfaces — and can quietly refuse to let the pilot exceed the aircraft's structural limits. The Airbus A320, introduced in 1988, was the first commercial airliner built around fly-by-wire from the start, a design philosophy Airbus has used across every aircraft family since.

None of this matters if everyone on board can't breathe. Air gets thin fast with altitude, and at a typical cruising altitude above 35,000 feet, it's not breathable at all. Pressurization systems pump compressed air into the cabin, keeping the pressure inside equivalent to somewhere between 6,000 and 8,000 feet — noticeably thinner than sea level, which is part of why flying leaves people more tired than the same hours spent on the ground, but perfectly breathable without supplemental oxygen.

An aircraft also needs power and air when its own engines aren't running, which is what the Auxiliary Power Unit is for: a small turbine, usually tucked into the tail, that supplies electricity and compressed air on the ground and serves as a backup power source in flight.
