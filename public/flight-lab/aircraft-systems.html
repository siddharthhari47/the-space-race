Aircraft Systems

Nobody looking at an aircraft from the outside thinks about its hydraulic lines. But move the yoke a few inches in the cockpit, and something several dozen feet away (a control surface out on the wing or tail) has to respond instantly, every time, for the whole flight. That's the job of the systems hiding underneath the skin.

Almost nothing critical on a commercial aircraft exists in a single copy. Hydraulics, electrical generators, even flight computers are duplicated or triplicated, each one capable of running the aircraft alone. Engineers don't design around the hope that a system won't fail. They design around the certainty that eventually, one will, and make sure that when it does, it's never the only story left running.

On early aircraft, moving the control column pulled a cable that physically moved a control surface — direct, mechanical, and limited by how hard a human could pull. Larger, faster aircraft needed more force than muscle could supply, so hydraulic systems took over: pressurized fluid doing the heavy work, with the pilot's input scaled up rather than applied directly.

Hydraulics still needed a human physically pulling on something, just with the effort multiplied. Fly-by-wire cut the mechanical linkage out of the loop entirely and replaced it with electronic signals: the pilot's inputs go to a flight computer, which decides how to move the control surfaces, and can quietly refuse to let the pilot exceed the aircraft's structural limits. The Airbus A320, introduced in 1988, was the first commercial airliner built around fly-by-wire from the start, a design philosophy Airbus has used across every aircraft family since.

All of that is pointless if the people inside can't breathe, and air gets thin fast with altitude. At a typical cruising altitude above 35,000 feet, it's not breathable at all. Pressurization systems pump compressed air into the cabin, keeping the pressure inside equivalent to somewhere between 6,000 and 8,000 feet, noticeably thinner than sea level, which is part of why flying leaves people more tired than the same hours spent on the ground, but perfectly breathable without supplemental oxygen.

An aircraft also needs power and air when its own engines aren't running, which is what the Auxiliary Power Unit is for: a small turbine, usually tucked into the tail, that supplies electricity and compressed air on the ground and serves as a backup power source in flight. It's also what actually starts the main engines in the first place. Nothing else on board is spinning fast enough to do it.

Naming the Wing's Moving Parts

An aircraft's wing looks like one solid shape from a distance, but almost every piece of its trailing edge is a separate control surface with its own job. The ailerons sit furthest out, near the wingtips, and handle roll: one goes down as the other goes up, so one wing makes more lift than the other and the aircraft banks. Move inboard from there, tucked between the ailerons and the fuselage, and you're looking at the flaps: hinged panels that extend to increase both lift and drag at low speed, which is why they come out for takeoff and landing and stay flush against the wing for the rest of the flight. There's more than one way to build a flap — a plain hinged flap is the simplest, a split flap only lowers the underside while the top stays put, and a Fowler flap does something cleverer still, sliding backward as it lowers so it increases the wing's actual area, not just its curvature. Some aircraft skip having separate ailerons and flaps altogether and use flaperons instead — one surface doing both jobs, an aileron and a flap folded into the same hinge line.

On top of the wing, spoilers do the opposite of a flap: they spoil lift on purpose, raising drag and dumping lift to enable a swift descent without picking up speed, and deploying fully once the wheels touch down to help kill lift and get the aircraft's weight onto its brakes. It's worth being precise about the name, since spoilers are not the same thing as airbrakes, even though both add drag. A dedicated airbrake, like the ones on some fighter aircraft mounted just behind the cockpit, exists purely to slow the aircraft down; many modern airliners combine both functions into one set of surfaces instead of carrying two. The wing's leading edge has its own devices too: slats that extend forward and down to improve lift at low speed and high angles of attack, either fixed in place or retractable to reduce drag once the aircraft is safely up to cruising speed, and Krueger flaps, which hinge a section of the lower wing surface out ahead of the leading edge instead.

The Tail, Properly Named

The empennage, the tail assembly, carries its own pair of control surfaces, and it's easy to mix up which does what if you're only going by "the tail moves." The vertical stabilizer is the fin itself, providing directional stability the same way feathers keep an arrow flying straight, and the rudder is the movable panel hinged to its trailing edge that actually controls yaw. The horizontal stabilizer is the small wing-like surface mounted to the empennage, keeping the aircraft balanced in pitch, and the elevator is the movable surface hinged to it that the pilot actually commands through the yoke. A trim tab, a smaller surface still, hinges off the elevator itself and exists purely to reduce the physical force the pilot has to hold on the yoke to maintain a given attitude. Trimmed correctly, an aircraft will hold a climb or a descent with almost no hand pressure at all. Not every aircraft sticks to one fin and one tailplane, either: twin-tail and V-tail layouts trade the single conventional fin for two smaller ones, and canard designs move a small horizontal surface out in front of the wing instead of behind it — different answers to the same balancing problem.

The Backup That Actually Worked

This page opened with the idea that engineers design around the certainty that something will eventually fail. The Ram Air Turbine is that idea built into hardware: a small propeller-driven generator, folded away out of the airflow until it's needed, that deploys into the airstream and generates power purely from ram pressure — the aircraft's own forward motion through the air, nothing electrical or hydraulic required to get it started. It exists for the scenario everything else on this page is designed to avoid: main engines gone, APU gone, and flight-critical instruments and control surfaces that still need power to keep the aircraft flyable.

On July 23, 1983, Air Canada Flight 143, a Boeing 767, lost both engines in mid-flight. Its RAT deployed, kept the hydraulics and instruments alive, and the crew glided the aircraft to a safe landing with everyone on board unhurt. A rather unglamorous folded-away turbine turned out to be the difference between a controlled glide and a catastrophe — exactly the kind of quiet redundancy this whole page has been about.
