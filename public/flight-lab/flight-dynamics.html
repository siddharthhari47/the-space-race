A·03

Flight Dynamics

Getting off the ground was never the hard part — plenty of people managed brief hops before 1903. Staying controlled once you are up there, and knowing exactly how hard you are allowed to pull, is the part nobody else had actually solved.

01

Three axes, all at once

An aircraft rotates around three independent axes, and controlling all three simultaneously is what separates flight from a controlled fall.

Pitch is the nose moving up or down. Roll is one wingtip dropping while the other rises. Yaw is the nose swinging left or right. Before the Wright brothers, most experimenters focused on lift and power and treated control as an afterthought — or, like Otto Lilienthal, solved it only partially, by shifting his own body weight the way a cyclist leans into a turn. It worked for gentle glides and did not scale to powered, sustained flight.

The Wrights' actual breakthrough was wing warping: twisting the wingtips in opposite directions using cables, rolling the aircraft on command. That single mechanical idea, refined into the aileron used on every aircraft since, is what made the 1903 Flyer a genuine aircraft rather than a powered glider that happened to survive its first flight.

Modern aircraft use three dedicated surfaces, one per axis: the elevator on the horizontal tail for pitch, ailerons near the wingtips for roll, the rudder on the vertical tail for yaw. A pilot rarely moves just one — turning an airliner coordinates aileron and rudder together, banking into the turn rather than skidding through it flat. You can rotate a real 777-300ER and click straight to each surface on the Interactive Aircraft Explorer.

02

How hard you may pull, and how fast

Load factor n = L/W is not free to be anything you like. A wing has to fly fast enough to make it, and a structure is only ever built to survive so much of it.

03

Three points, and which one you trust

An aircraft has three separate "centres," and confusing them is where most stability arguments go wrong.

The centre of gravity is where the aircraft's weight acts. It depends only on mass distribution — how the fuel, cargo and structure are arranged — and not at all on aerodynamics. The centre of pressure is where the net aerodynamic force acts instead, and it depends entirely on aerodynamics: shape and, critically, angle of attack. Raise the angle of attack on a cambered wing and the centre of pressure does not stay put — it moves forward, toward the nose.

That migration is the whole problem with using centre of pressure for a stability argument at all: a reference point that moves depending on how hard you are asking the wing to work is not a reference point you can design around. The fix is the neutral point — the one location, fixed by the aircraft's external geometry alone, where the pitching moment does not depend on angle of attack at all. It does not move when you pull back on the stick, which is exactly what makes it usable.

Put the centre of gravity ahead of the neutral point and a nose-up disturbance increases the tail's download faster than it increases the wing's lift, pitching the nose back down on its own — positive static stability, no pilot action required. Put the centre of gravity behind the neutral point and the same disturbance makes things worse, not better. The distance between the two, as a fraction of the wing's mean chord, is what pilots and engineers call the aircraft's static margin, and it is the single number that says how forgiving an aircraft is to fly.

Source: NPTEL course 101101079, Introduction to Aerospace Engineering — Flight, Prof. Rajkumar S. Pant (IIT Bombay), Lecture 51, "Introduction to Static Stability: Center of Pressure, Center of Gravity and Neutral Point."
04

How far, and for how long, are different questions

Both come from the same fuel tank, and both come from the Breguet equations — but they are maximised by flying at two different speeds.

For a jet, range is R = (V/ct)(L/D) ln(W₀/W₁) and endurance is E = (1/ct)(L/D) ln(W₀/W₁) — nearly the same expression, except range carries an extra factor of true airspeed V out front. That one factor is the whole difference: endurance only cares about burning fuel slowly, which means flying at the angle of attack that gives the best L/D outright. Range cares about covering distance per unit of fuel, which turns out to want a slightly different, faster operating point. A propeller aircraft has the opposite trade — its best-range speed is the one a jet would use for best endurance, and vice versa.

Range also depends on which way the wind is blowing, in a way pilots plan around explicitly: ground speed, not true airspeed, is what actually turns into distance. A tailwind adds directly to it and a headwind subtracts, so the same aircraft carrying the same fuel goes measurably further one way than the other on the same route.

The current world record for aircraft range is Steve Fossett's solo circumnavigation in the Scaled Composites GlobalFlyer, 41,467.46 km on 12 February 2006 — beating the previous record, Dick Rutan and Jeana Yeager's Rutan Voyager flight of 40,212.14 km on 23 December 1986. That same Voyager flight also holds a separate endurance record: 216 hours 3 minutes 44 seconds aloft, 14–23 December 1986, the first aircraft to circle the world without refuelling. The more recent endurance mark for a single leg belongs to Solar Impulse 2 — André Borschberg alone at the controls for 117 hours 51 minutes, Nagoya to Hawaii, 28 June–3 July 2015, on no fuel at all.

Source: NPTEL course 101101079, Introduction to Aerospace Engineering — Flight, Prof. Rajkumar S. Pant (IIT Bombay), Lecture 58 ("Range") and Lecture 60 ("Endurance"), part of a combined "Range & Endurance" session.
