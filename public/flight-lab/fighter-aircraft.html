What Makes a Fighter Different

Both aircraft in Flight Lab fly through the same atmosphere, obey the same four forces, and were designed by engineers solving the same equations. They look nothing alike. That's worth explaining.

A fighter's thrust-to-weight ratio above 1 means something genuinely strange: the engines push harder than gravity pulls. Point the nose at the sky and the aircraft keeps accelerating, with the wings contributing nothing at all. It is, for that moment, flying the way a rocket flies rather than the way an aeroplane does.

Start with the number that drives everything else. Thrust-to-weight is exactly what it sounds like: divide how hard the engines push by how much the aircraft weighs. A fully loaded airliner sits somewhere around 0.25, which is plenty. It only ever needs enough thrust to accelerate along a runway until the wings take over, then enough to balance drag for the next nine hours. An Su-35 at combat weight is above 1.

That single ratio reshapes the whole aircraft. If thrust is abundant, drag stops being the thing you optimise around, and you can afford shapes an airliner designer would reject immediately. A bubble canopy sitting proud of the fuselage. A wing small enough to be thrown around rather than large enough to be efficient. Weapons hung out in the airflow. Each of those costs fuel, and a fighter accepts the bill because the engines can cover it.

Where the Extra Thrust Comes From

Jet engines only burn a fraction of the air passing through them. Plenty of oxygen leaves out the back unused, and an afterburner is the blunt exploitation of that fact: spray raw fuel into the exhaust behind the turbine, light it, and the exhaust leaves faster than it otherwise would.

The gain is large and the cost is brutal. Roughly 50 to 65 percent more thrust, for fuel consumption that can more than double. Nobody cruises on afterburner. It gets used for takeoff, for accelerating through the transonic region, and for the few seconds of a fight where the alternative is losing. The nozzle has to physically open wider while it happens, which is why the petals at the back of a fighter visibly change diameter.

Pointing the Exhaust

Every control surface on a conventional aircraft works the same way: it sticks out into moving air and deflects it. That's a fine arrangement right up until there isn't enough moving air. At very low speed, or at very high angles of attack where the airflow has separated, a rudder or an elevator has progressively less to push against, and eventually the pilot is moving a stick connected to nothing useful.

Thrust vectoring sidesteps the problem entirely. If the nozzles themselves can swivel, the exhaust can be pointed off the centreline, and the resulting force doesn't care how fast the aircraft is travelling through the air. The engine is producing thrust regardless. Point it down and the tail goes down. Point the two nozzles in different directions and the aircraft rolls.

This is why those widely-spaced engines matter. Two nozzles close together on the centreline could push the tail up or down, but they'd have almost no leverage to roll the aircraft. Spread them apart and differential vectoring becomes a real roll control.

An Su-35 has three separate ways to roll: ailerons on the wings, tailplanes that can move differentially, and thrust vectoring. Redundancy is a side effect. The real reason is that each one stops working in a different part of the envelope, so between them there's always at least one that still bites.

Manoeuvrability isn't the only reason to build a vectoring nozzle. Point the exhaust far enough downward and thrust vectoring stops being about turning and starts being about not needing a runway at all — the F-35B's swivelling nozzle and lift fan give it a genuine vertical takeoff and landing capability, on an airframe that also has to cruise and fight like a conventional fighter the rest of the time.

Surfaces That Move as a Whole

There's a quieter difference at the tail. A 777 has a fixed horizontal stabilizer with a hinged elevator on the back of it, and that works well below the speed of sound. Past Mach 1 a shock wave forms on the surface and the hinged section loses much of its authority, because the airflow behind the shock barely responds to it.

Supersonic aircraft almost universally solve this the same way, by making the entire horizontal surface pivot instead of hinging a flap onto it. It's called an all-moving tailplane, or a stabilator. More pitch authority for the same area, and it keeps working where an elevator would have given up.

The Wing That Refuses to Stall

An airliner's wing spends its life at a few degrees of angle of attack, and that's by design. Flight Lab's Aerodynamics page covers what happens past the critical angle: airflow separates from the upper surface and lift collapses. Somewhere around 15 to 20 degrees for a conventional wing.

A fighter needs to operate well beyond that, so the wing is blended into the fuselage rather than bolted to it. Those blended extensions ahead of the wing root shed a strong vortex that runs back over the upper surface, and that vortex drags airflow back down onto the wing where it would otherwise have separated. It delays the stall dramatically. Controlled flight at 30 degrees of angle of attack is a normal part of this aircraft's envelope, not an emergency.

Two Answers to the Same Atmosphere

Put them side by side and the contrast is the point. A 777-300ER is 73.9 m long with a 64.8 m wingspan, carrying up to 396 people across an ocean on two enormous high-bypass engines tuned for efficiency. An Su-35 is 21.9 m long with a 15.3 m span, carrying one person, on two low-bypass engines tuned for thrust. You could lay three Su-35s nose to tail inside the airliner's length.

Neither is better. They were handed different problems. The airliner's problem is moving mass a long way cheaply, and every design decision follows from that. The fighter's problem is controlling energy fast, and every decision follows from that instead. Same air, same physics, same four forces. Completely different answers.

So here's the thing worth chewing on: if you had to design an aircraft that did both jobs reasonably well, which compromises would you make first?
