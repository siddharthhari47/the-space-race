Aircraft

An aeroplane is an argument about air. How much of it you have, how fast you move through it, and what it does to you when you run short. Every aircraft ever built is a different answer, and they all fit on one chart.

The two edges lean toward each other. Low down there is a wide band of usable speed; by 50,000 ft the slowest you may fly and the fastest you may fly have nearly converged. Run out of gap entirely and you are at the coffin corner, where slowing down stalls you and speeding up puts shockwaves on the wing.

The hollow markers are aircraft this envelope has no room for, and they miss it in both directions. Concorde, the MiG-25 and the SR-71 are off the right-hand edge — too fast. The Cessna and the Black Hawk are off the left, and that is the less obvious half: a loaded 777 would stall long before it slowed to their cruise speed. The U-2 is the interesting one. It is subsonic, and it gets to 70,000 ft by having an enormous wing carrying almost no weight, which drags its own stall boundary far enough left that there is still a gap up there for it to fly in.

One caveat worth drawing rather than hiding: this envelope only asks whether the wing can do it. It closes at about 57,000 ft, but a real 777 stops at 43,100 ft, and that lower line is on the chart too. The difference is engines — up there the air is too thin to make enough thrust, long before the aerodynamics give up.
