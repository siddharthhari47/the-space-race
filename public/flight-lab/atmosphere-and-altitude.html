Atmosphere & Altitude

Every aircraft on this site flies through air, and air is not the same thing twice. It thins with height, cools and warms in bands that don't follow any simple pattern, and changes with the weather and the latitude under you. Before any of the aerodynamics or flight dynamics on the rest of this site makes sense, it helps to be precise about the medium all of it is actually happening in.

The coldest air temperature ever recorded on Earth's surface was about -89°C, at Vostok Station in Antarctica. The thermosphere, more than a hundred kilometres up, regularly runs hotter than 1,500°C. And yet an astronaut floating through it wouldn't feel warm at all. The air up there is so thin that even though each molecule is moving fast (which is literally what a high temperature means), there are far too few of them hitting you to transfer any meaningful heat.

Aerospace vehicles split cleanly into two families depending on where they spend their working life. Atmospheric vehicles, helicopters and airplanes, do their job below the Kármán line, the altitude around 100 km that's conventionally treated as the edge of space. In practice, aircraft stay well under that: even the highest-flying ones rarely cross 15-20 km, because that's roughly where the air gets too thin for a wing or a jet engine to keep working properly. Above that line, air-breathing engines and aerodynamic lift stop being useful concepts at all, and you're in spacecraft and satellite territory instead: a different set of physics, covered in Space Lab.

Why We Pretend the Atmosphere Holds Still

Real air refuses to sit still for engineers. Temperature and pressure at a given altitude change with the weather, the season, and the latitude: the air over the equator behaves differently from the air over the poles on the same afternoon. That's a serious problem if you're trying to compare a wind-tunnel result, a flight test, and a design calculation, all of which might have been done on different days in different places. Aircraft performance depends directly on the atmosphere it's flying through, so without a shared reference, none of those three numbers could be honestly compared to each other.

The International Standard Atmosphere is the fix: a single, hypothetical, idealized atmosphere (a fixed sea-level temperature and pressure, and a defined way temperature changes with height) that has nothing to do with the actual weather outside on any given day. Nobody claims the ISA is what the air is really doing. It's a common ruler, so that a wing tested in Bangalore and a wing tested in Toronto can be compared on equal terms.

Three Ways to Measure "Up"

"Altitude" sounds like it should mean one simple number, but aerospace engineering uses several, because "how high" turns out to depend on what you're measuring height relative to. Geometric altitude is the straightforward one: physical height above mean sea level at your current location, measured as if the Earth's surface directly below you were the zero point. The catch is that "mean sea level" itself isn't one constant number. It varies by location, since the Earth isn't a perfect sphere and sea level rises and falls with local geography and gravity.

Absolute altitude accounts for the Earth's curvature explicitly, adding the planet's radius to the geometric altitude. It matters more the further you get from the surface, since the curvature is negligible for a cruising airliner but not for something in orbit. Geopotential altitude is the odd one out: it's a height defined so that it directly accounts for how gravity itself weakens as you climb, rather than treating gravity as constant the way the other two do. For anything flying through the atmosphere, that difference barely registers. It's really a space-vehicle concept, which is why atmospheric flight almost always just uses geometric altitude in practice.

The Layers Above You

The atmosphere isn't one uniform blanket of air. It's stacked in bands, and each one behaves differently with height. The troposphere is the one every aircraft on this site actually lives in: the lowest layer, where temperature drops steadily as you climb (roughly 6.5°C for every kilometre) and the air is well-mixed by weather, which is exactly why weather happens here and nowhere else. It ends around 11 km, at the tropopause, where the standard atmosphere's sea-level temperature of 15°C has cooled to around -56.5°C.

Above that is the stratosphere, and it does something that surprises most people the first time they hear it: temperature climbs back up with altitude instead of continuing to fall. The reason is the ozone layer, sitting inside the stratosphere, absorbing incoming solar ultraviolet radiation and re-radiating it as heat: a layer of gas acting as the atmosphere's own sunscreen, and warming itself in the process. Higher still is the mesosphere, the coldest layer of the entire atmosphere, dropping to roughly -90°C, and the layer where meteoroids entering from space burn up, since it's thick enough to generate real friction but far above anything an aircraft could reach. Above that is the thermosphere, genuinely strange in how it behaves: temperatures there climb past 1,500°C, but because the air is so thin, a tiny amount of absorbed solar energy is enough to swing that number dramatically — which is also why an object entering the atmosphere from space starts heating up seriously once it reaches this layer. The thermosphere overlaps with the ionosphere, the band where solar radiation strips electrons from atoms and leaves the air electrically charged; the two aren't separate layers stacked on top of each other so much as the same altitude range described by two different properties, temperature and ionization, that happen to track each other as you climb.

The Two Equations Everything Else Comes From

Almost every altitude-versus-pressure or altitude-versus-density relationship used in aerospace engineering traces back to two simple statements. The first is the hydrostatic equation: pressure decreases with height because there's simply less air stacked above you pressing down. The second is the ideal gas law, tying pressure, density, and temperature together. Combine the two and you can work out how pressure and density fall off through each atmospheric layer: a straightforward exponential decay in the isothermal layers, where temperature holds constant, and a slightly different relationship in the layers where temperature changes linearly with height instead. Even a property as unglamorous as air's own viscosity has to be modeled this way. It changes with temperature too, and formulas like Sutherland's law exist specifically to predict it at whatever altitude and temperature an aircraft or a wind-tunnel test actually needs.

What Your Altimeter Is Actually Telling You

None of this is purely academic. Pilots deal with a version of the "which altitude" problem every single flight, through altimeter settings identified by Q-codes. QFE sets the altimeter so it reads zero at a specific airfield's elevation, giving height above that particular field: useful in the airfield's own circuit, meaningless anywhere else. QNH sets it to read altitude above mean sea level instead, correcting for the local station pressure, which is what's used for most of a flight below the transition altitude. QNE is different again: it's the fixed international standard pressure setting, 1013.25 hectopascals, used by every aircraft at high altitude regardless of what the actual sea-level pressure happens to be underneath them that day. That shared, deliberately-not-locally-corrected reference is exactly what lets air traffic control keep aircraft from different regions safely separated in altitude — everyone is measuring "up" against the same fictional atmosphere, which is the ISA idea from the start of this page, still doing its job at 35,000 feet.
