Atmosphere & Altitude

Every aircraft is designed against an atmosphere that does not exist. The International Standard Atmosphere is a fiction agreed on by everybody, and that agreement is what lets two engineers on different continents compare performance figures at all.

01

What actually changes as you climb

Pressure and density fall away exponentially, and by the tropopause both are down to about a quarter of their sea-level value. Temperature does not follow them — it falls steadily to −56.5 °C at 11 km and then simply stops, holding flat for the next nine kilometres before starting to rise again.

That flat stretch is the tropopause, and it is the most useful altitude band in aviation. The air is thin enough that drag is a quarter of what it is at sea level, but the temperature has stopped dropping, so the speed of sound stops dropping too and the aircraft is not being pushed toward its critical Mach number by climbing further. Thin air, stable sound speed. That is why airliners cruise there and not somewhere else.

02

Two different speeds, one aeroplane

Hold the indicated airspeed fixed above and climb. Watch true airspeed walk away from it.

An airspeed indicator does not measure speed. It measures the pressure the oncoming air produces in a forward-facing tube, which is dynamic pressure — ½ρV². Because density is in that expression, the same reading means a very different speed depending on how thick the air is.

This is not a defect. Indicated airspeed is the number that matters for flying the aircraft: the wing stalls at a fixed indicated speed regardless of altitude, because both lift and the instrument depend on the same ½ρV². True airspeed is the number that matters for navigating. Pilots learn to think in both, and to know which question they are answering.

03

Where the air stops being survivable

Press the Armstrong preset. The number that matters there is not oxygen.

Most people assume the problem with altitude is running out of oxygen, and up to a point it is. But at 19.11 km the ambient pressure falls to 6.3 kPa, which is the vapour pressure of water at 37 °C. At that pressure the water in your eyes, your saliva and your lungs boils at body temperature. Breathing pure oxygen does not help, because the problem is no longer what you are breathing — it is the pressure itself.

That is the real reason the U-2 pilot and the astronaut wear the same kind of suit. Above the Armstrong limit, the distinction between high-altitude flight and spaceflight stops meaning very much — both are hostile in the same way, and both are solved the same way: carry your own pressure with you.

The line is named after Harry George Armstrong, a US Air Force physician who identified it in 1935 — nearly two decades before anyone flew high enough to have to care about it in practice.
