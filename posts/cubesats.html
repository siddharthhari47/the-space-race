What about CubeSats?

Siddharth Hariharan · Aug 5, 2026 · 2 min read

Everyone's heard of the Space Race and the big national programs, but there's a much smaller — literally — story running in parallel: the CubeSat.

A CubeSat is a satellite built to a strict standard: a single unit, or "1U," is just a 10 cm cube weighing around 1.33 kg. Need more room for instruments? Bolt a few units together — 2U, 3U, 6U, even 12U — and you've still got something you can hold with two hands, a world away from a multi-tonne geostationary satellite.

The standard was created in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari at California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs at Stanford, originally as a way to get university students building and launching real spacecraft instead of just simulating them on paper. To make that possible, Cal Poly also built the P-POD — the Poly Picosatellite Orbital Deployer — a spring-loaded tube that releases CubeSats like ammunition out of a magazine once their rocket reaches orbit.

That standardization is really the whole story. Before CubeSats, building a satellite meant custom engineering from scratch, and launching one could cost tens of millions of dollars. A CubeSat, riding as a secondary payload alongside someone else's mission, can get to orbit for a small fraction of that. Suddenly universities, startups, and even high schools could put something in space.

Here's roughly how the common sizes compare:

Unit Dimensions Typical Mass
1U 10 × 10 × 10 cm ~1.33 kg
3U 10 × 10 × 34 cm ~4 kg
6U 10 × 20 × 34 cm ~8 kg
12U 20 × 20 × 34 cm ~16 kg

They didn't stay small in ambition, either. In May 2018, two 6U CubeSats named MarCO-A and MarCO-B launched alongside NASA's InSight lander — and rode along all the way to Mars. They weren't part of the science mission; their job was to relay InSight's signals back to Earth during its nail-biting entry, descent, and landing. It worked, and the question of whether CubeSats could survive deep space was answered for good.

Closer to home, constellations of CubeSats now do work that used to require a handful of expensive satellites. Companies like Planet Labs operate fleets of small imaging satellites that photograph the Earth's entire landmass every day, tracking everything from deforestation to crop health to disaster response.

Of course, there are trade-offs. CubeSats are small, which means small solar panels, small batteries, and small antennas — and that limits power, data rates, and how long they last in orbit. Most CubeSats in low Earth orbit re-enter and burn up within a few years, which is arguably a feature: no expensive satellite loitering as space junk once its mission ends.

So — is the future of space exploration bigger rockets and bigger satellites, or thousands of tiny ones working together? Maybe it's both. Thank you for reading, and until next time.
