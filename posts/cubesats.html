What about CubeSats?

Siddharth Hariharan · Aug 5, 2026 · 2 min read

Space is not just giant rockets and multi billion dollar space stations. Over the last two decades, a much smaller kind of satellite has quietly become one of the biggest stories in the industry, the CubeSat.

A CubeSat follows a strict standard. One unit, called 1U, is just a 10cm cube weighing around 1.33kg. If a mission needs more room, engineers simply bolt more units together, so you get 2U, 3U, 6U and even 12U satellites. Even the largest of these is still small enough to carry in the back of a car.

This standard was created in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari at California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs at Stanford. The idea was simple, let university students actually build and launch real spacecraft instead of only doing the calculations on paper. Cal Poly also built the P-POD, the Poly Picosatellite Orbital Deployer, a spring loaded tube that pushes CubeSats out into orbit once their rocket gets there.

Before this standard existed, building a satellite meant starting from scratch every time, and launching one could cost tens of millions of dollars. A CubeSat rides along as a secondary payload on someone else's rocket, so it reaches orbit for a fraction of that cost. This is why universities, small startups and even high schools have been able to build and launch their own satellites.

Here is a comparison of the common CubeSat sizes.

Unit Dimensions Typical Mass
1U 10 × 10 × 10 cm ~1.33 kg
3U 10 × 10 × 34 cm ~4 kg
6U 10 × 20 × 34 cm ~8 kg
12U 20 × 20 × 34 cm ~16 kg

CubeSats have not stayed small in ambition though. In May 2018, two 6U CubeSats called MarCO-A and MarCO-B launched together with NASA's InSight lander, and travelled all the way to Mars. They were not part of the actual science mission, their job was to relay InSight's signals back to Earth during its entry, descent and landing. It worked, and proved that CubeSats could survive the journey through deep space.

Closer to home, companies like Planet Labs now run entire fleets of small CubeSats that photograph the whole of Earth's landmass every single day, tracking things like deforestation, crop health and disaster response, work that used to need a handful of much larger and more expensive satellites.

CubeSats do have their limits. Being this small means small solar panels, small batteries and small antennas, which limits how much power they have, how fast they can send data, and how long they last in orbit. Most CubeSats in low Earth orbit only last a few years before burning up on re-entry. Some would say that is actually a good thing, since it means there is no expensive dead satellite left floating around as space junk.

So will the future of space exploration be bigger rockets and bigger satellites, or thousands of tiny ones working together? Maybe you already have your own opinion on which one wins. Thank you for reading, and until next time.
