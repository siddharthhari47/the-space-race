What about CubeSats?

Siddharth Hariharan · Aug 5, 2026 · 2 min read

The Space Race did not end on 20 July 1969, the day Neil Armstrong stepped onto the Moon. It just changed shape. Back then it was two superpowers racing to plant a flag. Today it is universities, startups and countries of every size racing to design, build and launch their own spacecraft, and a huge part of that new race runs through something the size of a shoebox: the CubeSat.

A CubeSat follows a strict standard. One unit, called 1U, is just a 10cm cube weighing around 1.33kg. If a mission needs more room, engineers simply bolt more units together, so you get 2U, 3U, 6U and even 12U satellites. Even the largest of these is still small enough to carry in the back of a car, which is exactly the point. Small is what let this race open up to almost anyone.

Did You Know?

The CubeSat standard was created in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari at California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs at Stanford. The goal was to let university students build and launch real spacecraft instead of only doing the calculations on paper. Cal Poly also built the P-POD, the Poly Picosatellite Orbital Deployer, a spring loaded tube that pushes CubeSats out into orbit once their rocket gets there.

Before this standard existed, building a satellite meant starting from scratch every time, and launching one could cost tens of millions of dollars, a price tag only a handful of governments could ever afford. A CubeSat rides along as a secondary payload on someone else's rocket, so it reaches orbit for a fraction of that cost. That single change is what turned space exploration from a two country contest into a genuine race between universities, startups and small nations.

Universities were the first to take advantage, building CubeSats as student projects that actually fly. Startups followed, using CubeSats to test business ideas in orbit before committing to anything bigger. Small nations that could never have afforded a traditional satellite programme have used CubeSats to launch their first ever spacecraft, often with help from international university partnerships such as Japan's BIRDS Project, which has flown first satellites for countries including Ghana, Bhutan and Sri Lanka. For the first time, having a small budget is not enough to keep you out of the race.

Here is a comparison of the common CubeSat sizes.

Unit Dimensions Typical Mass
1U 10 × 10 × 10 cm ~1.33 kg
3U 10 × 10 × 34 cm ~4 kg
6U 10 × 20 × 34 cm ~8 kg
12U 20 × 20 × 34 cm ~16 kg
Engineering Insight

Every extra unit is a trade-off. More units means more room for batteries, instruments and propulsion, but also more mass, more drag in low orbit and a bigger, pricier slot to buy on a rocket. A lot of CubeSat engineering is really just deciding what to leave out.

If you were designing a mission today, what would you build? An Earth observation camera, a communications relay, or a small sensor meant to prove new technology works before it flies somewhere far riskier? That last option is exactly what took CubeSats furthest from home.

In May 2018, two 6U CubeSats called MarCO-A and MarCO-B launched together with NASA's InSight lander, and travelled all the way to Mars. They were not part of the actual science mission, their job was to relay InSight's signals back to Earth during its entry, descent and landing. It worked, and proved that CubeSats could survive the journey through deep space, opening up an entirely new front in the race: deep space technology demonstration on a budget.

Closer to home, companies like Planet Labs now run entire fleets of small CubeSats that photograph the whole of Earth's landmass every single day, tracking things like deforestation, crop health and disaster response, work that used to need a handful of much larger and more expensive satellites.

Most CubeSats in low Earth orbit only last a few years before burning up on re-entry. Some would say that is actually a good thing, since it means there is no expensive dead satellite left floating around as space junk once its mission is over.

Why CubeSats Matter in the Modern Space Race

Lower launch costs

Riding as a secondary payload turned a tens-of-millions-of-dollars problem into one universities and startups can actually afford.

Rapid innovation

A CubeSat can go from idea to orbit in a couple of years instead of a couple of decades, so new ideas get tested in space much faster.

Student-built missions

Students now design, build and operate real spacecraft as part of their education, not just simulations of one.

Earth observation

Constellations of small satellites photograph the planet daily, tracking climate, agriculture and disasters as they happen.

Communications

Small satellites now relay data and connectivity from orbit, work once reserved for satellites the size of a bus.

Deep-space technology demonstrations

Missions like MarCO proved that small, low cost hardware can survive far beyond Earth orbit, opening the door to cheaper deep space missions.

So will the future of space exploration be bigger rockets and bigger satellites, or thousands of tiny ones working together? Maybe it is already both, and maybe you will be one of the engineers deciding which one wins. Thank you for reading, and until next time.
